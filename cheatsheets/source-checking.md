# Source checking: a cautious conclusion

Airbotix original sharing sheet · 19 October 2026 · suggested Years 7–12 by reading level. This fictional exercise teaches reading and reasoning; no real school survey is reported.

| Question | Action |
|---|---|
| Who is speaking? | Identify the author or organisation and the purpose of the text. |
| What is the claim? | Write the exact proposition being argued. |
| What supports it? | Locate the data, observation or attributed source; distinguish it from opinion. |
| Who is represented? | Check the sample, selection process and missing groups. |
| When and where? | Check dates and whether the context matches the claim. |
| What remains uncertain? | Name the limitation before generalising. |

## Complete example

> A school of 600 pupils invited volunteers to answer one lunchtime question. Thirty replied. Eighteen preferred a quiet lunch area. A newsletter headline read: “Our students want quiet lunches.”

The evidence establishes that 18 of the 30 respondents, or 60%, preferred a quiet area. It does not establish that 60% of all 600 pupils prefer it. The respondents volunteered at one lunchtime, and their year groups are not identified. A better conclusion is: “Most respondents in this small voluntary survey preferred a quiet lunch area.” Before a school-wide decision, gather a broader sample and ask about alternative preferences. A second account is useful only if it adds independent evidence, not if it repeats the same survey.

**Final check:** claim → evidence → explanation → limitation. Finding a polished website or a repeated claim is not a substitute for reading its source.
