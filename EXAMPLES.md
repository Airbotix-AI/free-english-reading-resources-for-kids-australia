# Complete original reading examples

These are complete original editorial examples, with fictional situations and model answers, not real pupil work or assessment results. They are not formally levelled or guaranteed decodable. Use only when the vocabulary and taught sounds fit the reader. Suggested stages are flexible; older English learners can use an accessible task without being assigned a younger school year.

## Early reading: a small event

> Sam has a red bag. The bag tips. A pen falls out. Sam puts it back.

1. Who has the red bag?
2. What happens after the bag tips?

Model answers: Sam has the bag. A pen falls out after the bag tips. The sentence “Sam puts it back” tells us he returns the pen. Read the text again to check the sequence.

## Primary reading: inference with evidence

> Mia left her umbrella by the classroom door. Water dripped onto the mat. She checked the clock, then sat down with her book instead of walking to the bus stop. She chose a seat away from the wet window.

1. Why does Mia probably stay inside? Give two clues.
2. Does the text prove that the bus is late?

Model answers: She probably stays out of the rain: the umbrella drips, and she sits away from the wet window. The text does not establish a late bus; checking the clock could mean several things. Separate a supported inference from a fact explicitly stated.

## Teen reading: claim, evidence and limitation

> A school newsletter says, ‘Students want a quiet lunch space.’ Its evidence is a voluntary survey of 30 pupils at one lunchtime. Eighteen respondents preferred a quiet space and twelve preferred a music area. The school has 600 pupils. The newsletter does not say which year groups answered or how the invitation was shared.

1. What claim can the survey reasonably support?
2. What prevents a conclusion about every pupil?

Model answers: Eighteen of the 30 respondents preferred a quiet lunch space. The survey does not establish the preference of all 600 pupils: participation was voluntary and occurred at one lunchtime. A broader sample across year groups and days would improve the evidence. A cautious conclusion is “Most respondents preferred a quiet space”, with the sample described.
